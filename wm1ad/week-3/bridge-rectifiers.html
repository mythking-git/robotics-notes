Bridge rectifiers

Bridge rectifiers

Properties1
tagswm1ad, week-3

21 Oct 20264 min read

The idea

A diode is a one-way valve for current. In the symbol, current can only flow the way the triangle points, and getting through costs a small, roughly fixed voltage, the forward voltage VF​≈1 V. Backwards, it blocks, up to its limit VRRM​.

One diode on its own (a half-wave rectifier) lets the positive half of the AC through and throws the negative half away. A full bridge uses four diodes arranged in a diamond so that both halves come out positive.

How the bridge works

Name the four corners: A and B connect to the AC, + and − connect to the load.

DiodeAllows current
D1A → +
D2B → +
D3− → A
D4− → B

Two arrows point into +, two point out of −. So whatever the AC does, current can only arrive at + and leave from −. It always goes through the load from + to −.

Positive half (A pushes): A → D1 → + → load → − → D4 → B. D2 and D3 would need current to flow against their arrows, so they block.

Negative half (B pushes): B → D2 → + → load → − → D3 → A. Same direction through the load.

The two things to remember

  1. Current always passes through exactly two diodes. So the output peak is Vpk​−2VF​, and two diodes are always making heat.
  2. The output is a row of positive humps, two per AC cycle: 100 per second at 50 Hz.

A three-phase bridge (6 diodes) on a three-phase supply gives six overlapping humps per cycle, which is nearly flat DC. That’s what big industrial kit uses.

Smoothing with a capacitor

The humps still drop to 0 V between peaks. A capacitor across the output works like a water tank: it fills at each peak, and the load drains it a little until the next hump refills it. The dip is the ripple, ΔV.

Deriving the ripple:

  1. Refills happen 100 times a second, so the gap is Δt=2Fgrid​1​=10 ms.
  2. In that gap the load takes charge ΔQ=IΔt (current is charge per second).
  3. For a capacitor Q=CV, so the voltage drops by ΔV=ΔQ/C.

ΔV=2Fgrid​CI​​

The capacitor charges to the top of each hump, minus the two diode drops:

VC,max​=Vpk​−2VF​

Heat in the bridge

  1. Power = voltage × current. A conducting diode has a fixed VF​ across it, so P=VF​×i at every instant.
  2. Averaged over time, VF​ is constant, so P=VF​×Iavg​. Diodes use average current, unlike resistors and MOSFETs, which use I2R with RMS.
  3. Two diodes are always on, so P=2VF​Iavg​.
  4. You’re usually given RMS, and for these humps Iavg​=0.9IRMS​ (see AC values).

PD(FB)​=2×0.9×VF​ID,RMS​=1.8VF​ID,RMS​​

Choosing a bridge from its datasheet

CheckRuleWhy
IF(AV)​Above your current, with margin (~50%)It sets how much average current it can carry without overheating
VRRM​Above the peak, 325 V on UK mains, with marginWhen a diode is off, it has to block the full peak
VF​Lower is betterLess heat, slightly higher output

Worked example

A robot charger uses a GBPC12 bridge (VF​=1.1 V, IF(AV)​=12 A, VRRM​=800 V) on 230 V mains. It draws 2 A RMS and has a 4700 µF smoothing capacitor. Find the bridge’s heat, the capacitor’s peak voltage and the ripple. Is the bridge suitable?

Answer

PD(FB)​=1.8×1.1×2=3.96 W VC,max​=325.3−2(1.1)=323.1 V ΔV=2×50×4700×10−62​=0.472​=4.26 V Suitable: 12 A ≫ 2 A, and 800 V > 325 V.

Common mistakes

  • Forgetting the two diode drops: it’s Vpk​−2VF​, not −VF​.
  • Forgetting µF is ×10−6. 4700 µF = 0.0047 F.
  • Using 50 instead of 2×50 in the ripple formula. A full bridge refills twice per cycle.
  • Checking VRRM​ against 230 V instead of the 325 V peak.

Check yourself

1. In the positive half, why doesn't current go A → D3?

D3 only allows − → A. Going A → − would be against its arrow, so it blocks.

2. You double C. What happens to the ripple?

It halves, because ΔV∝1/C.

3. A bridge with VF​=0.9 V carries 3 A RMS. How much heat does it make?

1.8×0.9×3=4.86 W.
